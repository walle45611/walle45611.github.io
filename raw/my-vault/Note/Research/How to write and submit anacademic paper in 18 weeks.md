
# Introduction and Literature Review Writing Notes

## 1. Introduction

The introduction should provide only a brief overview of the whole paper. It should not give too many details.

It should include the topic, motivation, problem, rationale, purpose, research questions or objectives, method or approach, and a brief outline of the paper structure.

The introduction is usually written toward the end of the writing process, like the abstract.

The introduction should avoid unnecessary technical jargon. If technical terms are necessary, they should be briefly defined.

Academic English does not always require passive voice. In the introduction and discussion, writers should generally prefer active voice because it makes arguments and claims clearer.

---

## 2. Problem, Rationale, and Purpose

These three ideas should be distinguished clearly.

**Problem** means what happened or what issue exists.

**Rationale** means why the issue is important.

**Purpose** means how the study addresses the issue from the researcher’s point of view.

A simple logic is:

**Problem → Rationale → Purpose**

---

## 3. Research Questions and Objectives

A paper should clearly state its research questions and objectives.

Useful guiding questions include:

1. What question does this paper aim to answer?
    
2. What does this study aim to achieve?
    
3. In what context was the study conducted?
    
4. What method or approach was used?
    

The research question should be specific, not too broad.

---

## 4. Literature Review

The literature review should be concise, relevant, and synthesized.

It should not be a comprehensive review of the entire field.

It should include:

1. The position and significance of the study in the broader field
    
2. Definitions of key concepts and research constructs
    
3. Synthesis of relevant previous findings
    
4. Existing methods and evaluation approaches
    
5. Theoretical support for the research questions, objectives, or hypotheses
    
6. Knowledge gaps, inconsistencies, and controversies
    
7. The main contribution of the study
    

The literature review should not be written as a study-by-study summary.

---

## 5. Knowledge Gap

A literature review should explain what is still missing, unclear, inconsistent, or controversial in previous studies.

A knowledge gap may include a lack of research, inconsistent findings, controversies, limitations of existing methods, or a missing connection between concepts.

The main contribution of the study should be connected to the knowledge gap.

---

## 6. Definitions and Technical Terms

Do not copy definitions directly from previous research.

Definitions should be adapted to the context of the current study.

Technical terms should be defined when they first appear.

Definitions should not be listed one by one in the literature review.

---

## 7. Attributes, Factors, and Opposing Viewpoints

Attributes, factors, elements, or issues should not be merely listed in the literature review.

They should be explained, compared, and synthesized.

Opposing viewpoints should also be briefly explained, and the reasons for accepting or rejecting them should be stated.

---

## 8. Citation Problems

Avoid the following citation problems:

1. Misattributing sources
    
2. Citing one source repeatedly
    
3. Over-citing basic definitions
    
4. Treating implications as facts
    
5. Using too many direct quotations
    

Page numbers should not be used unnecessarily.

---

## 9. Literature Review Structure

The literature review should be organized by topics, themes, or specific issues.

Avoid one-word headings.

For example, instead of:

**Knowledge Graph**

Use:

**Knowledge Graphs for Clinical Knowledge Organization**

Relevant diagrams can be used when they help compare approaches or explain relationships.

---

## 10. Tense and Voice

Use **present tense** for general facts and accepted knowledge.

Example:  
“Clinical decision-support systems require interpretable reasoning.”

Use **present perfect tense** for previous research that is still relevant.

Example:  
“Previous studies have examined large language models in clinical decision support.”

Use **past tense** for specific studies or completed methods.

Example:  
“Smith et al. proposed a retrieval-based framework.”

Use **passive voice** only when the performer is unknown, irrelevant, obvious, or less important than the action.

---

## 11. Better Academic Expressions

“This study addresses the problem of...”  
比  
“This paper investigates...”  
更能凸顯研究問題。

其他常用句型：

“This study examines...”  
“This study investigates...”  
“This study proposes...”  
“This study addresses...”  
“This study aims to...”

---

## 12. Core Logic

The main logic of academic writing is:

**Problem → Rationale → Purpose → Literature Review → Knowledge Gap → Research Question → Method/Approach → Contribution**
